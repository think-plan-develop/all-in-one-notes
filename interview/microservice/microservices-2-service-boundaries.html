1Monolith vs Microservices

A monolith packages an application's main capabilities into one deployable unit. A modular monolith keeps those capabilities separated in code while still deploying the application together. Microservices split a system into independently deployable services organized around business capabilities.

MonolithOne application and deployment. It is straightforward to develop and operate, but teams and releases share the same runtime and scaling boundary.
MicroservicesMultiple services with explicit business ownership and independent deployment. They enable selective scaling and releases, at the cost of distributed-system complexity.

How to choose

ConsiderationMonolith / modular monolithMicroservices
Best fitEarly products, small teams, tightly related capabilitiesStable business boundaries, multiple teams, independent scaling or release needs
DeploymentDeploy the application as one unitDeploy each service independently
DataCan share one database and transactionsEach service owns its data; cross-service workflows need APIs or events
OperationsFewer network and infrastructure concernsRequires service discovery, observability, resilience, and distributed consistency
Practical guidanceStart with a well-structured modular monolith unless independent ownership, scaling, or delivery needs justify the operational cost of splitting services. Microservices are not an automatic upgrade.

Interview-ready summary

Choose the architecture based on business boundaries, team ownership, deployment needs, and operational maturity. A modular monolith provides clear internal boundaries without network and distributed-data costs; split a capability into a service when its independent evolution provides more value than the added complexity.

🧭 Service Boundaries in Microservices

A service boundary defines what a microservice is responsible for, which business logic and data it owns, and how it communicates with other services.

2.1Divide by Business Capability

Divide microservices based on business responsibilities, not technical layers (controllers, models, database queries).

🔐

Auth Service

Login, registration, authentication

OwnsUsers, sessions
🏠

Property Service

Property registration and management

OwnsProperties
📄

Document Service

Upload, verification, document management

OwnsDocuments
⭐

Review Service

Review assignment, approval, rejection

OwnsReviews, decisions
💳

Payments and refunds

OwnsPayments, refunds
🔔

Notification Service

Email, SMS, alerts

OwnsNotification logs
RuleEvery service should have one clearly defined business responsibility.
2.2Bounded Context ⭐ Very important

A bounded context is a business area where terms, rules, and data models have a specific meaning.

🔐 Auth Service

User means login credentials, account status, authentication.

⭐ Review Service

Reviewer means assigned reviews, approval permissions, availability.

Both can refer to the same person using userId, but maintain their own business models.

RememberOne bounded context does not necessarily mean one microservice.
2.3High Cohesion vs Low Coupling ⭐ Very important
High cohesion+Low coupling=✅ Ideal architecture
🧲

High cohesion

Keep related business logic together

ExampleReview creation, approval, rejection, history in Review Service
🔌

Low coupling

Minimize dependencies between services

ExampleReview calls Property API instead of accessing Property DB
🧩

Low cohesion

Unrelated features grouped together

ExampleAuthentication and payment processing in one service
⛓️

High coupling

Services depend heavily on each other

ExampleReview needs 5 Property API calls for one operation
2.4Database Ownership

Each service owns and modifies its own data.

❌ Incorrect

flow
Review Service → Property Database

✅ Correct

flow
Review Service → Property API → Property Database

Example: when a lawyer approves a review

  1. Review Service updates review status and decision history in its database.
  2. Review Service publishes ReviewApproved event.
  3. Property Service consumes the event and updates its own data if required.
BenefitIndependent data ownership and reduced coupling. Related changes within Review can remain in one local transaction.
2.5When Should We Split a Service?
QuestionWhy it matters
Does it have a separate business responsibility?Clear ownership
Does it own specific data?Avoid shared database access
Can it deploy independently?Independent releases
Does it need independent scaling?Better resource utilization
Does it frequently depend on another service?Identify tight coupling
Do related operations require one transaction?Keep atomic operations together
Does splitting justify additional complexity?Avoid unnecessary microservices
ExampleCreate a separate Review Service if it has complex workflows, independent deployment needs, or dedicated ownership. Otherwise, keep it inside Property or Conveyancing Service.

2.7Interview-Ready Questions
QQ1. How do you identify microservice boundaries?▼
By analyzing business capabilities, bounded contexts, data ownership, high cohesion, low coupling, and independent deployment requirements.
QQ2. What is the difference between cohesion and coupling?▼
  • Cohesion measures how closely related operations within a service are.
  • Coupling measures how dependent services are on each other.
QQ3. What is a bounded context?▼
A clearly defined business area where domain concepts and rules have consistent meaning.
QQ4. Can two microservices share a database?▼
Yes, but they should maintain clear data ownership. Directly modifying another service's tables or collections should be avoided.
QQ5. When should two features remain in one microservice?▼
When they share tightly related business rules, require frequent atomic updates, or splitting would introduce excessive network communication.
